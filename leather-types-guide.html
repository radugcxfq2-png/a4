Guide One

The Leather Types Guide

Two bags can both be labelled “genuine leather” and have almost nothing in common. Grade, finish and tanning decide how a bag looks, ages and needs to be treated.

Start with the grade

A hide is thick, and it is split into layers. Which layer a bag is cut from is the first thing to understand.

  • Full-grain uses the top layer with its surface untouched. The fibres are densest here, so it is the strongest and longest-lived. Natural marks are part of its character.
  • Top-grain is the same layer with the surface lightly sanded to remove blemishes, then refinished. Smoother and more uniform, a little less durable.
  • Corrected grain is sanded more heavily and embossed with an artificial grain pattern, then sealed with pigment. Consistent and practical.
  • Split leather comes from the lower layers. It is used for suede, or coated to imitate the grades above.

The phrase “genuine leather” means only that the material is leather. It says nothing about which of these it is.

Then the finish

Finish is what has been done to the surface, and it matters as much as grade for everyday care.

Aniline

Dyed through with transparent dye and left unsealed. You can see every pore. It feels warm and soft, absorbs water and oils readily, and develops a deep patina.

Semi-aniline

The same, with a thin protective coat. Most of the natural look, with some resistance to spills.

Pigmented

Coated with an opaque colour layer. The surface is even and tough. Most pebbled leathers are finished this way.

Saffiano

A cross-hatch pattern pressed in under heat and sealed with wax or resin. Rigid, scratch-resistant and almost waterproof.

Patent

A high-gloss lacquer coat. Wipes clean, but can pick up colour from other items and is hard to repair once scuffed.

Vegetable or chrome tanned

Tanning turns raw hide into leather. Vegetable tanning uses plant extracts over several weeks. The result is firm, smells sweet and earthy, and darkens noticeably with sunlight and handling. Chrome tanning takes days, and gives softer, more supple leather that holds bright colours and resists water better. Many bags combine both.

Suede and nubuck

Both have a raised nap. Nubuck is top-grain leather sanded on the outer side; it is fine, strong and expensive. Suede is the underside of a split; it is softer and more open. Neither should be wiped with a damp cloth or treated with cream. They are brushed, and protected with a spray made for napped leather.

How to tell what you have

  1. Look closely. Irregular pores and small marks suggest full-grain. A perfectly repeating pattern suggests embossing.
  2. Press with a thumb. Natural leather wrinkles finely around the pressure point, like skin.
  3. Try a water drop somewhere hidden. If it soaks in and darkens, the leather is unsealed. If it beads, there is a protective coat.
  4. Check the edge. A raw edge with a fibrous look shows the real structure.
