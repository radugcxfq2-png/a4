The Stain Care Guide

With leather, the first few minutes decide the outcome. The right response is usually gentle and patient, and the wrong one is nearly always rubbing.

Three rules before anything else

  1. Blot, never rub. Rubbing drives a stain deeper and damages the finish.
  2. Test first. Try any product on a hidden area, such as inside a flap, and wait until it is dry.
  3. Know when to stop. If two gentle attempts have not worked, a third harsher one will not. Take it to a specialist.

Water marks

Rain leaves rings because the wet area dries with a visible edge. Remove the edge by evening out the moisture. Dampen a soft cloth with clean water, wring it well, and wipe the entire panel from seam to seam. Stuff the bag with paper to hold its shape and let it dry at room temperature for a full day. Never use a radiator or hairdryer, which hardens and cracks leather. Condition lightly once it is completely dry.

Grease and oil

Blot any excess with a dry cloth. Cover the mark with a generous layer of plain cornflour or unscented talc and leave it overnight. The powder draws the oil out. Brush it away gently the next day and repeat if a shadow remains. Do not add water; oil and water together set the stain. On unsealed leather, a faint mark often blends into the patina over the following weeks.

Ink

Ink is the hardest stain to treat at home. On finished, coated leather, a fresh ballpoint line can sometimes be lifted with a leather ink-remover stick, used exactly as directed. On aniline leather and suede, home treatment usually spreads the ink and removes colour. Leave it and consult a leather specialist as soon as possible; fresh ink is far easier for them than old ink.

Make-up

Lift solid residue with the edge of a card. On finished leather, wipe with a cloth barely dampened with water and a drop of leather cleaner, working from the outside of the mark inward. On suede, let it dry fully, then use a suede eraser block and brush the nap back up.

Colour transfer

Dark denim and new dark clothing often leave a blue or grey shadow on pale bags. On coated leather, a leather cleaner on a soft cloth removes most of it if treated early. On untreated leather the dye bonds quickly and usually needs professional cleaning. Prevention is easier: apply a suitable protector to pale bags, and carry them on the side away from new jeans.

Mould

Grey or white bloom means the bag has been stored damp. Take it outdoors, brush the surface with a soft dry brush, then wipe with a cloth lightly dampened with clean water. Dry thoroughly in moving air, out of direct sun. Air the lining too. Store it afterwards in a breathable dust bag in a dry room; mould returns wherever it is damp.

What never to use

AvoidWhy
Baby wipes, household wipesSolvents and detergents strip the finish
Nail-varnish remover, hairsprayDissolves dye and coating
Washing-up liquid, soapToo alkaline; dries and stiffens leather
Heat of any kindShrinks and cracks the fibres
Olive or cooking oilDarkens unevenly and turns rancid
